Vacant Estate Home: Insurance & Security

A home sitting empty during probate or a committee process carries real risk that most standard home insurance policies don't actually cover.

The short version: most standard homeowner insurance policies restrict or void coverage once a home has been vacant for a set period — commonly somewhere around 30 days, though this varies by insurer and policy. If a home sits empty during probate, a committeeship process, or simply while a family sorts out what to do next, this needs active attention, not an afterthought.

Why vacancy changes the insurance picture

Insurers treat vacant homes as meaningfully higher risk — nobody is there to notice a burst pipe, a break-in, a roof leak, or a small fire before it becomes a major loss. Standard policies are priced and underwritten assuming regular occupancy, and many include a vacancy clause that reduces or eliminates coverage once the home has been empty past a certain point. This is worth flagging to the existing insurer immediately once a home becomes vacant — not months later when a claim is being made and the policy turns out not to have actually been in force.

What executors and committees typically need to do

  • Notify the existing insurer that the home is now vacant, and ask directly whether coverage is affected
  • Consider a dedicated vacant home insurance policy, designed specifically for this situation — these exist precisely because standard policies often don't adequately cover an empty property
  • Arrange regular check-ins — a neighbour, a property manager, or regular visits by the executor/committee — since many vacant-home policies require evidence of regular inspection to remain valid
  • Address obvious risk factors: shutting off water if appropriate for the season, securing the property, maintaining basic yard upkeep so the home doesn't visibly read as abandoned

Why this matters for the sale itself: a neglected vacant home doesn't just carry insurance risk — it can lose real market value if it sits visibly empty and untended while probate or a court process plays out. Mark Jontz routinely advises families on practical steps to protect both the insurance position and the home's eventual sale value during this waiting period, since the two are more connected than people expect.

Timing with the eventual sale

Because probate and committeeship processes can take real time, a home may need to sit vacant for months before a sale can proceed. Getting the insurance situation sorted properly at the start avoids a much worse problem later — discovering a claim won't be honoured after something's already gone wrong.

Common Questions

Vacant Estate Home: Insurance & Security — FAQ

How long can a home sit vacant before standard insurance is affected?
This varies by insurer and specific policy, but many standard homeowner policies restrict or void coverage once a home has been empty for roughly 30 days or so. Check directly with the existing insurer as soon as the home becomes vacant — don't assume the standard policy still fully applies.
Do I need a special insurance policy for a vacant estate home?
Often, yes. Dedicated vacant home insurance policies exist specifically because standard homeowner coverage frequently doesn't adequately protect an empty property. Worth discussing with an insurance broker once you know the home will sit vacant for any meaningful length of time.
What practical steps should I take for a vacant estate home?
Notify the existing insurer immediately, look into vacant-home-specific coverage, arrange regular check-ins on the property, and address basic risk factors like securing the home and keeping up appearances so it doesn't read as abandoned.
Does a vacant home affect its eventual sale price?
It can — a visibly untended, empty property can lose curb appeal and raise buyer concerns about deferred maintenance. Keeping a vacant estate home reasonably maintained during probate or a court process protects both the insurance position and the eventual sale value.
Who's responsible for maintaining a vacant estate home during probate?
Generally the executor (or the Committee, in a committeeship situation), as part of their broader duty to manage the estate's assets responsibly. This is a real, practical responsibility, not just a legal formality.
