My Parent Died. What Do I Do With Their House?

You don't need to have this figured out today. Here's roughly what actually happens next, before you talk to anyone else.

The short version: the house almost certainly can't be sold right away, and that's normal, not a problem you caused. What happens next depends on two things: whether your parent left a will, and how the property was titled. Figuring those two things out comes before anything else — including before you worry about cleaning, repairs, or listing.

First, figure out how the house was owned

If your parent owned the home jointly with a spouse (as joint tenants, the common arrangement for married couples), the house typically passes directly to the surviving spouse without going through probate. If your parent owned the home alone — which is common for a widowed or single parent — the house generally has to go through probate before it can be legally sold or transferred, regardless of what the will says should happen to it.

Then, find out if there's a will

If there's a will, it names an executor — the person legally responsible for settling the estate, including the house. That person applies to the BC Supreme Court for a Grant of Probate. If there's no will, a close relative usually applies to become the estate's administrator instead — see our guide to dying without a will in BC for how that process differs.

What this means for the timeline

Whether there's a will or not, the court process takes real time — commonly a few months, sometimes longer for a complicated estate. During that period, the house generally sits as-is: it's not usually something you're expected to deal with urgently, and in many cases it can be listed for sale before the process fully completes, with the sale timed around it. Our realistic timeline guide walks through what that actually looks like start to finish.

You don't have to figure all of this out alone. Mark Jontz has walked families through exactly this situation hundreds of times — what applies to your specific case, what to do first, and when it's actually time to think about selling. A direct conversation early on tends to save a lot of guessing later.

A rough order of what to deal with

  • Confirm whether there's a will, and who it names as executor
  • Confirm how the property was titled (sole name, joint tenancy, or tenants in common)
  • Talk to an estate lawyer or notary about whether probate applies
  • Secure the property — insurance, locks, mail — while things get sorted
  • When the time is right, talk to someone who handles estate sales specifically about the house itself

Common Questions

My Parent Died — FAQ

Can I sell my parent's house right away?
Usually not immediately. If the house was in your parent's name alone, it generally can't be legally sold or transferred until probate (or administration, if there's no will) is granted by the BC Supreme Court. If it was held jointly with a spouse, it may pass directly to the surviving owner instead. Which applies depends entirely on how title was held.
Do I need to go through probate?
It depends on how the property was titled and whether there's a will. Property held solely in the deceased's name almost always requires probate before it can be sold. Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship typically doesn't. See our full probate guide for the details.
Who's responsible for dealing with the house?
If there's a will, the executor named in it. If there's no will, a close relative usually applies to the court to become the estate's administrator. Either way, that person needs legal authority from the court before acting on real estate in most cases.
What should I do first?
Find out if there's a will, confirm how the property was titled, and talk to an estate lawyer or notary about whether probate applies to your situation. You don't need to have the house sale figured out on day one — getting the legal authority sorted comes first.
