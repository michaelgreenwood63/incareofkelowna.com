Preparing an Estate Home for Sale

Clearing out, repairing, and getting an inherited or estate home ready to list — what actually needs to happen, and in what order.

The honest version: for most families, the decision to sell isn't the hard part — facing a home full of someone's entire life is. Mark Jontz walks estate properties specifically with this in mind, and helps families sequence the practical work so it doesn't feel as overwhelming as it looks on day one.

Step 1: Sort before you clear

Before anything gets removed in bulk, go through the home methodically for anything with real financial or sentimental value: jewelry, documents (wills, insurance papers, financial records), family photos, heirlooms, and anything that might be worth appraising separately. This should happen before a clean-out crew or estate liquidator touches anything — once items are boxed and gone, there's no going back.

Step 2: Decide what gets kept, sold, donated, or discarded

Most families find it easier to sort belongings into four simple categories rather than agonizing item by item: keep (family members want it), sell (has resale value — estate sale, consignment, online), donate (usable but not worth selling), and discard. Doing this in writing, especially with multiple beneficiaries involved, avoids disputes later about who took what.

Step 3: Clear the home

Once sorting is done, the bulk clear-out can happen — hired junk removal, an estate sale company, or a combination. Mark can connect families with trusted local professionals who handle this kind of full-home clear-out regularly, so it's one less thing to coordinate yourself.

Step 4: Assess what actually needs repair

Estate homes are often lived-in for decades, which means deferred maintenance is common. The question isn't "what would make this look perfect" — it's "what would meaningfully affect value, safety, or a buyer's financing." A pre-listing walkthrough with a realtor experienced in estate sales specifically helps separate must-fix issues (safety hazards, major systems failures, anything that would flag on an inspection) from cosmetic items that rarely return their cost on a home you're not living in.

Step 5: Decide on staging vs. as-is

Full staging isn't always worth the cost or effort for an estate sale — sometimes a clean, empty, well-lit home shows perfectly well on its own. This is a case-by-case call based on the property, the market, and the timeline the estate is working with.

Step 6: Handle the practical stuff — insurance, utilities, security

A vacant estate home needs its own attention: most standard home insurance policies change or lapse when a property sits unoccupied for an extended period, so confirming vacant-home coverage with the insurer is an early, easy-to-miss step. Utilities, lawn care, and basic security (especially over a longer probate timeline) also need someone responsible for them.

Common Questions

Preparing an Estate Home for Sale — FAQ

Do I need to clear out the home before listing it?
Not always, but it usually helps. Buyers and their lenders need to be able to inspect the home properly, and a home packed with decades of belongings is harder to photograph, show, and appraise accurately. A full clear-out isn't always required before the first showing, but it's almost always required before closing.
Should repairs be done before listing an estate home?
Only the ones that genuinely affect value or dealbreak a sale — safety issues, major deferred maintenance, anything that would show up badly on an inspection. Mark Jontz walks every estate property specifically to separate "fix this first" from "let the next buyer handle it," since over-investing in a home you don't personally plan to live in rarely pays for itself.
Who pays for clearing out and repairing the home?
Generally these are legitimate estate expenses, reimbursable from the estate before distribution to beneficiaries — but this should be confirmed with the estate's lawyer or accountant, since recordkeeping matters for the final accounting.
What about sentimental or valuable items mixed in with everything else?
Worth sorting before any bulk clear-out begins, not during it — family heirlooms, documents, and anything of real or sentimental value should be identified and set aside first. This is usually one of the more emotionally difficult parts of the process, and worth giving real time to rather than rushing.
